💡 WHAT THIS PROJECT IS In Chapters 5 and 6 you watched machines do astonishing things with images and language — and in both chapters, the same uncomfortable pattern surfaced: these systems are only as good as the data they learned from, and that data is rarely fair. A facial-recognition system trained mostly on lighter skin tones works worse on darker ones. A language model trained mostly on standard English performs worse on AAVE, Spanglish, or regional dialect. These aren't glitches — they are fairness problems, predictable from how the systems were built. In this project, you will document one real case of AI bias, surface a limit yourself in a system you can use, and reflect honestly on who is harmed, who is responsible, and what habit you will carry forward.

PART 1A Case Study in AI Bias / 10 points

✅ DO THIS Find one real, well-documented case of bias in an AI system — computer vision (facial recognition, image search, content moderation, medical imaging) or language (translation, chatbot, automated hiring, sentiment analysis). It must be a case that actually happened, reported by a credible source — a news outlet, an academic paper, a civil rights organization, a government audit. Cite at least one source by name and link.
💡 WHERE TO LOOK Good starting places — none of these are endorsements, just places to find documented cases:
  • The Algorithmic Justice League (ajl.org) — published reports on facial recognition bias
  • The AI Incident Database (incidentdatabase.ai) — searchable archive of real cases
  • Major news outlets — reporting on AI deployment failures (NYT, WaPo, Reuters, ProPublica)
  • Academic papers — e.g., the 2018 "Gender Shades" study by Buolamwini and Gebru
  • Government audits — e.g., NIST evaluations of facial recognition systems
Do not invent a case. Do not paraphrase from a forum post or social media. Use a real, citable source.
⚠️ COVER ALL FOUR POINTS
  1. What happened? Describe the case briefly.
  2. Who was affected? Be specific — which group of people, in what context, with what consequence.
  3. Why did it happen? Use vocabulary from Chapter 5 or Chapter 6 — training data, embeddings, decision boundary, out-of-distribution, generalization. Show you understand the mechanism, not just the headline.
  4. What was the response? Did the company fix it? Was there regulation? Did anything change? "Nothing changed" is a legitimate answer if it's true.

PART 2Hands-On — Surface a Limit Yourself / 10 points

✅ DO THIS Pick one path below — vision or language — and run 5 systematic tests on a system you can actually use. The point is to push deliberately along one dimension and see where the system handles it well and where it stumbles. Honest results matter more than clean narratives. If your tests show the system works fine, that is also a finding — write that up.

Path A — Vision

Use the HF Spaces object-detection model from GRP06 (huggingface.co/spaces/Xenova/yolov9-web) or any other free vision tool. Run 5 images that systematically vary along one dimension:

  • 5 portraits across a range of skin tones (use stock photos, your own, or family photos with permission)
  • 5 everyday objects from different cultures (Cuban café cup vs. plain coffee mug, piragua cart vs. ice-cream truck, mango on the tree vs. apple on a counter, dominos vs. playing cards)
  • 5 photos of the same scene at 5 different times of day (varying lighting)
  • 5 photos of common workplace items in 5 different settings (home office, garage, food truck, salon, school)
  • Your own systematic variation — describe the dimension

Path B — Language

Use Gemini (gemini.google.com), your GRP07 Gem, or any other LLM you can access. Send the same request in 5 different language registers — choose registers you can use authentically (don't fake AAVE, Spanglish, or a dialect you don't actually use). Examples:

  • The same question in Standard English / Casual English / Texting style / Spanglish / Spanish
  • The same résumé feedback request in formal English / AAVE / casual English / Caribbean English / a creole or pidgin you know
  • The same legal question with vocabulary at a college level / 8th-grade level / casual / dialect / non-English
  • The same emotional support message in 5 registers
  • Your own systematic variation — describe the dimension

PART 3Ethics, Responsibility & What You'll Carry Forward / 10 points

✅ DO THIS Write 300–350 words addressing the three questions below. Don't list — argue. Pick a position on each question and defend it.
💡 THE THREE QUESTIONS
  1. Who is harmed? When the kinds of biases from Parts 1 and 2 show up in a deployed system, who actually feels the consequence? Name a specific person, group, or scenario — not "people in general."
  2. Who is responsible? The training data? The developers who built the model? The company that deployed it? The user who relied on it? Make an argument about where the weight of responsibility should sit, and why.
  3. What habit will YOU carry forward? Looking at your career, your daily life, or a role you imagine taking — what one habit from this work (testing for limits, demanding provenance, questioning fluency, checking sources, reading guarantees critically) will you bring with you, and where specifically will you apply it?

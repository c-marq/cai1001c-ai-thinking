GRP06 — Stress-Test a Vision Model

4 points Group of 3–4 ~40 minutes
Session 7 · Chapter 5: Computer Vision  |  Featured tool: Hugging Face Spaces  |  Due: end of class
💡 WHAT THIS LAB IS Tonight your group puts a real object-detection model to work — and then tries to break it on purpose. You will pick a real-world use case (accessibility, security, retail, traffic, healthcare, or wildlife), feed it ten of your own images, and decide whether the model is fit for that purpose. Honest evaluation is the point — not a perfect score. The Space is free, runs in the browser, and needs no account.

Group members & roles

Every member picks one role. All roles contribute — no coding is required for any of them.

RoleMember name
Lead Driver — runs the Space on the shared screen and uploads images
Data Interpreter — reads and records the model's outputs and confidence scores
Presenter — delivers the share-out to the class
QA Reviewer — stress-tests the diagnosis and fills this worksheet

1Pick a use case and open the Space

✅ DO THIS — 5 min As a group, pick one real-world use case for an object-detection model. The model you'll use is trained on COCO — about 80 everyday categories like people, vehicles, animals, household items, and sports equipment. Pick a use case it might plausibly serve:
  • Accessibility — a smartphone app that describes a scene out loud to a person with low vision.
  • Security / safety — a school-hallway camera flagging unattended bags.
  • Retail — a self-checkout camera identifying produce on the belt.
  • Traffic / city ops — a downtown camera counting cars, buses, and bicycles.
  • Healthcare — a clinic camera checking the contents of a supply shelf.
  • Wildlife / environment — a Florida camera trap identifying animals.
Then open the Space your instructor posted in the Zoom chat — primary: huggingface.co/spaces/Xenova/yolov9-web. It runs in your browser; give it 10 seconds to load the model the first time.
💡 WHY ARE WE DOING THIS? A vision model is excellent at the things it was trained on and useless at the things it has never seen. Naming a use case forces you to evaluate the model in context — "is it good enough for the job we'd ask it to do?" — instead of cheering an impressive number. That is the habit Section 4.4 and Section 5.2 want you to carry forward into every model from now on.

2Run 5 clear images and record

✅ DO THIS — 10 min As a group, feed the Space five clear, ordinary images from a member's phone — the kind of photo a normal user of your use case would upload. For each, write down what the model said (its top label or labels), the confidence score, and whether the answer was right for your use case.
Image (one-line description)Model's top label(s)ConfidenceRight for our use case? (Y/N + 1 line)
💡 WHY ARE WE DOING THIS? Five clear examples establish a baseline. If the model already fails on easy photos in your use case, you can stop — it isn't the right tool. If it succeeds on the easy ones, you've earned the right to ask the harder question next: where does it break, and does that matter?

3Now try to break it on purpose

✅ DO THIS — 15 min Feed the Space five hard images. Push deliberately on at least three of the stress dimensions below — note which one you pushed on in column 2:
  • Unusual angle — from above, from below, extreme close-up.
  • Poor lighting — backlit, deep shadow, low light, glare.
  • Partial occlusion — the subject is half hidden behind something.
  • Out-of-distribution subject — something COCO probably wasn't trained on (a Florida iguana, a guava, a piragua cart, a Cuban café cup, a specific cultural object).
  • Cluttered scene — many overlapping objects, busy background.
  • Ambiguous case — could plausibly be two categories.
Image (one-line description)What we pushed onConfidenceWhat it missed or got wrong
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE Picking "hard" images that are also tiny, hopelessly blurry, or just bad photos. A model failing on a 50-pixel-wide smudge teaches no one anything. Hard means clear-enough that a person reads it fine, but with one stress dimension pushed on. The chapter's point is that a model can fail on images you read instantly.
🛑 STOP AND CHECK Before Step 4, your two tables have ten rows of real outputs and confidence numbers — actual labels the Space produced, not "I think it would say…" If a row is blank or guessed, go fix it.

4Diagnose one failure using Chapter 5

✅ DO THIS — 5 min Pick the most interesting failure from your tables — not the worst, the most instructive. As a group, write three sentences explaining why it failed, using vocabulary from Chapters 4 and 5. Reach for the right words: pixels-as-numbers, training data, confidence score, decision boundary, out-of-distribution, overfitting, generalization, bias in training data.
💡 WHY ARE WE DOING THIS? "It got it wrong" is not a diagnosis. "It got it wrong because [chapter language]" is the skill this chapter is teaching. Naming why a model fails is the difference between a person who knows what AI does and a person who knows what it is.

5Verdict and share-out prep

✅ DO THIS — 5 min As a group, answer the verdict honestly. Your Presenter delivers a 60-second report to the class.
🛑 STOP AND CHECK Your verdict isn't a single word. "Yes" or "no" alone is not a verdict — why is the verdict. If the answer fits on one line, you haven't argued it yet.

What to submit

💡 ONE WORKSHEET PER GROUP This completed worksheet is your submission. It must include your use case, your ten image observations, your three-sentence diagnosis, and your verdict.
✅ HOW TO SUBMIT In your browser, choose File → Print → Save as PDF. Confirm both tables and all text fields are visible — nothing cut off — then upload the PDF to the GRP06 assignment in Canvas. One submission per group, with every member's name in the roles table. Due by the end of class.

Troubleshooting

ProblemFix
The Space won't load, or shows an error.Refresh once. If it's still down, your instructor will post the fallback Space link in chat — switch to that and keep going.
The model reports nothing on what looks like a clear image.It only detects ~80 COCO categories. If your subject isn't one of them (a guava, a piragua, an iguana), it may not appear at all — and that is a finding worth recording, not a problem to fix.
The bounding box covers half the image, or two objects in one.A confident wrong answer. Record it as a failure mode — exactly the kind of mistake a deployed model needs a human to catch.
The model reads 99% confidence on a clearly wrong answer.Section 4.4: confidence and correctness are not the same thing. Record both numbers and use this image as your Step 4 diagnosis — it is the chapter's lesson made visible.
The group can't agree on whether an output is "right."Go back to your Step 1 use case. Right is a context question. 60% confidence on "person" might be plenty for crowd-counting and nowhere near enough for a security alert.
Uploading from a phone is slow.Have one member AirDrop or email the photos to the Lead Driver, who uploads from a laptop. Faster than uploading one at a time from phones.
